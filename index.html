World Models for Physical AI: Learning to Predict, Plan, and Act in the Real World

A critical survey of predictive world models for embodied agents. We trace how prediction enters simulation, policy learning, planning, and execution-time correction — and examine the experimental evidence for when predictive capability actually improves downstream decisions, and when it does not.

Abstract

What this survey argues

World models have become increasingly important for Physical AI by predicting how environments evolve under actions. They support robot learning, simulation, planning, and control, yet existing research remains fragmented across predictive representations, system architectures, and evaluation protocols. We review their data foundations, modeling approaches, and integration with learning and decision-making systems, covering robotics, navigation, and autonomous driving.

Through comparative analysis of experimental evidence, we examine when predictive capabilities translate into practical benefits and where current evaluations remain insufficient. The organising claim is deliberately narrow: a gain in prediction fidelity does not establish a gain in decision utility, and the evidence that would show otherwise is often missing from the reported experiment rather than merely inconclusive.

Three cross-study findings guide the analysis.

01

Fidelity ≠ planning utility

Recursive fidelity and planning utility require different validation criteria. In GNS, training with perturbed inputs improves recursive rollout accuracy while worsening clean one-step accuracy. In ContactWorld, the representation with the lowest relative prediction-error growth does not yield the highest planning success.

02

Evaluator ≠ learner

Evaluator agreement does not reliably predict policy-training utility. DexTouch-WM reports higher evaluator correlation for WM-Mix, while WM-Robot yields higher reported training means for two of three policy learners. IWS likewise finds learner-dependent outcomes from synthetic demonstrations under a fixed data budget.

Correction ≠ one latency effect

Execution-time correction depends on temporal alignment and access to predictive references. FutureRTC shows action-index choice depends on the prediction context's time origin; TacPAC shows cached tactile expectations can improve correction while its Bottle task remains unchanged at 40%.

Part I — Foundations and Scope

Prediction, action, and use

A world model can be situated along three descriptive axes before its performance is assessed. These describe an interface, not a ranking of model families or a newly exhaustive taxonomy.

A1

Prediction target

Identifies the future quantity: pixels or video, latent state, objects and geometry, or physical and multimodal variables.

A2

Action query

Asks whether the predictor is passive, responds to supplied candidate commands, generates a desired future, or jointly produces actions and futures.

A3

Prediction consumer

Identifies where the forecast enters computation: simulation, policy learning, policy evaluation, planning and selection, or execution-time monitoring and correction.

Working definitions. A world model is a learned predictor of environmental evolution — future observations, latent states, object relations, physical quantities, or action consequences. An action-conditioned model predicts consequences under specified actions or interventions; passive models need not expose executable-action queries. Prediction, policy, and control are computational roles, not necessarily separate networks: action generation alone does not establish consequence prediction.

Part II — Data, Representations, and Predictive Models

From observed motion to executed actions

Predicting observed motion and predicting a robot command's consequence require different information. The comparison follows what is available for simulation, learning, and action selection — not representation richness alone.

2.1 Data: the intervention gap

Passive video, synchronized commands, physical-state labels, and recovery annotations supply different supervision for action queries, policy updates, and interaction outcomes. Synchronized corpora establish the command–observation link without erasing control conventions: DROID broadens scenes on shared hardware, while Open X-Embodiment pools bodies with coarsely aligned end-effector actions but retains frame and control differences.

A shared storage format does not itself align executable command meanings. Action frame, duration, control mode, observation frequency, and embodiment remain part of the supervision — otherwise pooling more trajectories can change which transition a training pair describes rather than simply broadening the situations covered.

2.2 Visual simulation as a feedback loop

Returning generated observations to an agent changes the consumer, even when the predictor stays the same. In Interactive World Simulator, teleoperators supply commands to collect synthetic demonstrations, whereas policy evaluation feeds generated observations back to a policy.

The first interface creates training examples; the second lets the tested policy choose its subsequent inputs to the simulator. Neither use can be reduced to the fidelity of an independently sampled video.

2.3 Latent and structured predictive representations

A representation matters through the distinctions its consumer needs: derivatives for an integrator, attainable geometric change for inverse dynamics, or anticipated deformation for contact control. The relevant path is state acquisition → predicted evolution → the operation receiving that prediction.

A compact encoder is not yet a queryable transition. LeVJEPA aligns global/local views with a shared encoder and block-causal attention, but adds no action-conditioned successor model; matched-pretraining-FLOP probing improves Kinetics-400 while lowering Something-Something-v2 accuracy relative to V-JEPA 2. With supplied physical state, graphs organise the numerical update rather than discover objects: GNS predicts particle accelerations and integrates them, and MeshGraphNets combines mesh and spatial-contact connectivity.

2.4 Generalization across scenes and embodiments

Scene, task, sensor, and body changes stress different interfaces. Three bridges can coexist — transferring experience, adapting executable actions, and preserving selected outputs under observation changes — but their evidence concerns different changes and consumers rather than a single generalization score.

Sim-to-real WAM adapts Cosmos Policy with approximately 800 simulated demonstrations per task and visual domain randomization, reporting 35% mean zero-shot success across four Franka manipulation tasks, without real policy demonstrations or real-data finetuning. This measures the complete transfer recipe — including video pretraining, generated demonstrations, and domain randomization — rather than an isolated contribution of world-model prediction.

Part III — World Models in Learning, Simulation, and Decision Making

Where the prediction actually enters

Predictions can change command selection, policy parameters, action decoding, or correction after new observations. A simulator supplies interactions; an evaluator estimates behavior quality. We compare these operations rather than treating their benefits as interchangeable.

3.1 Planning with predicted consequences

Consequence planning must both propose a useful motion and recognise its value before committing. PETS propagates sampled trajectories through probabilistic dynamics ensembles and uses CEM to search for high-reward sequences; PlaNet infers a latent belief and runs CEM on predicted latent states and rewards without rendering images for scoring; TD-MPC2 guides sample-based latent search with a learned policy prior and a terminal value.

Ensemble uncertainty, a visual belief, and policy/value guidance change different parts of the decision — rather than merely offering alternative forecast formats.

3.2 Driving and navigation

Driving and navigation distinguish three interfaces: searching supplied candidates, conditioning action generation on predicted context, and refreshing decisions from feedback. In the first, prediction changes a ranking; in the second, it supplies features or training targets; in the third, the next computation is anchored either to generated history or to the executed world.

These distinctions determine which ablation explains a reported gain — not merely which system reports the higher score.

3.3 Policy learning with imagined experience

What the optimizer consumes determines how imagination changes learning. Original Dreamer differentiates imagined returns through fixed latent dynamics; MBPO supplies short predicted state/reward transitions from real replay to SAC updates; DreamerV3 instead uses normalized REINFORCE. PWM retains first-order return gradients and trains per-task actors from a shared multi-task model.

MBPO's Hopper ablations retain much of the benefit with one-step branches, degrade with long branches, and outperform extra no-model SAC updates. Transition accumulation, return truncation, and state information are different weaknesses — these controls establish neither a common optimal horizon nor equal total compute.

3.4 Memory, failure detection, and recovery

Memory preserves context; recovery changes what happens after a failure. A history-conditioned forecast can support simulation, and a history-conditioned actor can maintain task progress, without either constructing a response to an impending failure.

Mem-World retrieves observations from action-conditioned, wrist-view-centred 4D memory for simulation; in its separate policy-evaluation experiment, imagined and real policy success correlate at Pearson r = 0.97. This is evaluator agreement, not an online recovery endpoint.

3.5 Visual plans and joint world–action models

Predictive supervision and runtime future information answer different questions. UniPi generates task-conditioned video, then grounds frame transitions through an independently trained inverse-dynamics decoder; its reported experiments execute open-loop. Fast-WAM makes the stage distinction explicit: its base action tokens read features of the current observation, not future-video tokens, and removing video co-training leaves architecture/inference unchanged while costing 8.0 RoboTwin and 4.1 LIBERO points.

Executing only decoded actions does not remove future tokens from sampling. Information available during denoising and outputs retained after denoising are therefore distinct descriptions of the same system.

Boundary case — reasoner-assisted revision. Reasoner-assisted revision changes a policy proposal by interpreting observations and task constraints; it need not evaluate a predicted trajectory. Astra corrects proposals, reporting 48% hybrid versus 26% direct-Astra success on ten selected RoboDojo tasks. The decision interface is complementary to prediction, not a substitute for it.

Part IV — Evaluation, Safety, and Reliability

What a result actually establishes

Evaluation requires a separate map of what a result establishes. We distinguish six evidence dimensions, adapted from evaluation questions across prior world-model and embodied-AI surveys. These are claim-dependent checks, not six mandatory sequential gates or a single aggregate score.

L1 — Perceptual fidelity

Tests visual coherence of the predicted future.

L2 — State and physical fidelity

Tests task-relevant dynamics, geometry, or contact state.

L3 — Action realization

Checks whether changing a supplied command produces the corresponding robot or agent motion.

L4 — Interaction grounding

Checks whether the realized motion produces the corresponding environmental response, including object displacement or contact.

L5 — Planning and policy utility

Measures effects on action choice or learning under a defined consumer and protocol.

L6 — Operational reliability and safety

Measures failures, intervention timing, recovery, and constraints during execution.

The chain to watch: commanded action → realized motion → environment response → new observation. An L1 or L2 gain does not establish an L5 benefit, and a policy-evaluation correlation is not a newly trained policy's success rate.

4.2 Uncertainty, constraints, and runtime guardrails

Safety mechanisms intervene at different operations: exploration, policy optimisation, command acceptance, or alarm generation. Classical certificates establish a response under explicit assumptions — confidence-bounded Lyapunov learning restricts exploration to an invariant region; predictive safety filtering tests a backup trajectory; a control-barrier-function QP constrains its instantaneous derivative.

Their guarantees require a valid state/uncertainty model and, respectively, an initially stabilising policy, a feasible terminal backup, or an admissible safe input. A visual forecast supplies none of these physical interfaces automatically.

4.3 Operational reliability and attribution

A runtime response requires information to arrive before the relevant action becomes committed. FutureRTC's Table 3 isolates action-index alignment while holding an execution-time oracle context fixed: using the first generated action yields 96.6% success, whereas starting at index k = 20 after the same delay yields 0.9%.

This is an oracle-context diagnostic, not evidence that the model can access that future observation when it generates the action. Intervention can itself cause failure: VLA-FixBench's automatic judgers achieve 0–25% completion versus 30% policy-only and 65% human intervention on two physical Franka tasks.

Synthesis

Three consumer-dependent validation choices

The evidence supports narrower, consumer-specific claims rather than a universal ranking. Table 7 separates observations by consumer (A) from unresolved cross-cutting explanations and proposed tests (B).

GNS's recursive robustness cannot replace ContactWorld's goal-scored planning endpoint; DexTouch-WM's evaluator ranking does not select a training-data generator independently of the learner, as IWS's mixtures also illustrate; and FutureRTC's action-index alignment differs from TacPAC's access to a predicted tactile reference.

Existing surveys establish the broader evaluation and attribution principles. The contribution here is to connect specific controls and adverse outcomes to these distinct choices — and to be explicit about which follow-up tests have not been run.

What the unexecuted tests would settle

Case A — candidate availability, prediction, and selection. An oracle branch can show that an alternative in the supplied pool would have succeeded; trajectory-error recovery can still fail to improve the executed policy. Neither result measures outcomes for candidates that were never generated or selected.

Case B — representation versus goal geometry. A discriminating follow-up would freeze a compatible encoder, target and goal metric; match sensing, capacity, data and search; and vary predictive contact input separately from scoring access. Predictor-only gains would weaken a scoring-only account.

Case C — prediction, feedback, and executable action windows. A common-implementation test could fix policy, training, compute allowance, acquisition schedule and committed prefix, then independently vary fixed-context refinement, fresh observations and delay — distinguishing an information limit, inaccurate anticipation, or a closed action window.

Open problems

What this survey cannot settle

Each item is phrased as a testable gap rather than a wish — the form the survey argues future work should adopt.

Measurement, not capability

Rejected candidates' physical outcomes are unobserved. Until a protocol branches reproducible failure states while fixing candidate set, continuation policy, horizon and stochastic repeats, selection regret cannot be estimated from executed runs alone.

Learner-dependent utility

Evaluator agreement does not select a training-data generator independently of the learner. IWS's mixtures and DexTouch-WM's three learners differ in direction, so a single correlation statistic cannot stand in for the downstream recipe.

Runtime endpoints are mixed

Correction access, action-index alignment, and update timing are distinct operations. None of the reported studies collapses them into a single runtime-efficiency claim, and the paper declines to do so in synthesis.

Guarantees need physical interfaces

Formal certificates assume a valid state/uncertainty model plus a stabilising policy, a feasible terminal backup, or an admissible safe input. A visual forecast supplies none of these automatically.

Intervention can cause harm

Automatic repair pipelines scored below policy-only baselines on physical Franka tasks. Reliability therefore requires measuring the consequence of an intervention, not only its timing.

Sampling representativeness

The Core Evidence Set is mechanism-focused, not a representative sample. Prevalence of benefits and failures across the field cannot be inferred from it, and the inclusion rationales were recorded retrospectively.
