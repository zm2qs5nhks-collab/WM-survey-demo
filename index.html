World Models for Physical AI: Learning to Predict, Plan, and Act in the Real World

Abstract

Better predictions, unclear decisions

World models, which predict how environments evolve under actions, increasingly support simulation, policy learning, planning, and evaluation in physical AI. However, differences in representations, action interfaces, and evaluation protocols obscure when better predictions yield better decisions. This survey reviews data foundations, modelling approaches, and applications in robotics, navigation, and autonomous driving.

Drawing on experiments and counterexamples, we examine gaps between recursive accuracy and planning utility, evaluator agreement and policy-training outcomes, and predictive information and execution-time correction. We organise evidence across five uses, identify unresolved attribution questions, and discuss requirements for reliable, action-grounded world modelling.

Part I — Foundations and Scope

Three axes before any performance claim

A world model can be situated along three descriptive axes. These describe an interface, not a ranking of model families or a newly exhaustive taxonomy.

A1

Prediction target

What is predicted: pixels or video, latent state, objects and geometry, or physical and multimodal variables.

A2

Action query

Whether the predictor is passive, answers supplied candidate commands, generates a desired future, or jointly produces actions and futures.

A3

Prediction consumer

Where the forecast enters computation: simulation, policy learning, policy evaluation, planning and selection, or execution-time correction.

The action query is the axis that gets confused

Output modality does not tell you what a model can be asked. Four representative cases, each described in the manuscript, show why the interface has to be read separately from the architecture:

Answers a supplied action

V-JEPA 2 freezes an action-free pretrained encoder and trains a predictor on synchronized robot observations and base-frame end-effector changes, supporting planning over candidate actions.

action-conditioned

Generates a desired future

UniPi generates task-conditioned video, then grounds frame transitions through a separately trained inverse-dynamics decoder. Its reported experiments execute open-loop.

goal → future

Advances a supplied physical state

GNS predicts particle accelerations and integrates them from supplied states, rebuilding proximity as particles move — no new candidate-command queries.

structured state

Passive latent dynamics

Dreamer trains an actor on returns imagined in latent space. The prediction is real; an executable action query is not part of the interface.

passive

Working definition. A world model is a learned predictor of environmental evolution — future observations, latent states, object relations, physical quantities, or action consequences. Prediction, policy, and control are computational roles, not necessarily separate networks: action generation alone does not establish consequence prediction.

Part II — Data, Representations, and Predictive Models

What supervision actually licenses

Predicting observed motion and predicting a robot command's consequence require different information. The comparison follows what is available for simulation, learning, and action selection — not representation richness alone.

2.1 The intervention gap in data

Passive video, synchronized commands, physical-state labels, and recovery annotations supply different supervision. Synchronized corpora establish the command–observation link without erasing control conventions: DROID broadens scenes on shared hardware, while Open X-Embodiment pools bodies with coarsely aligned end-effector actions but retains frame and control differences.

A shared storage format does not itself align executable command meanings. Action frame, duration, control mode, observation frequency, and embodiment remain part of the supervision — otherwise pooling more trajectories changes which transition a training pair describes rather than simply broadening coverage.

2.2 Video as feedback, not just forecast

Returning generated observations to an agent changes the consumer even when the predictor is unchanged. In Interactive World Simulator, teleoperators supply commands to collect synthetic demonstrations, whereas policy evaluation feeds generated observations back to a policy.

The first interface creates training examples; the second lets the tested policy choose its subsequent inputs. Neither use can be reduced to the fidelity of an independently sampled video.

2.3 Latent and structured representations

A representation matters through the distinctions its consumer needs: derivatives for an integrator, attainable geometric change for inverse dynamics, or anticipated deformation for contact control. The relevant path is state acquisition → predicted evolution → the operation receiving that prediction.

A compact encoder is not yet a queryable transition. With supplied physical state, graphs organise a numerical update rather than discover objects: MeshGraphNets combines mesh and spatial-contact connectivity and integrates node outputs, optionally remeshing. Hybrid models retain an analytical backbone, fitting friction or base parameters alongside learned residuals — the correction changes simulated object evolution, not the policy's command.

2.4 Generalization across scenes and embodiments

Three bridges can coexist — transferring experience, adapting executable actions, and preserving selected outputs under observation changes — but their evidence concerns different changes and consumers rather than a single generalization score.

Sim-to-real WAM adapts Cosmos Policy with roughly 800 simulated demonstrations per task and visual domain randomization, reporting 35% mean zero-shot success across four Franka manipulation tasks. That measures the complete transfer recipe — video pretraining, generated demonstrations, and randomization together — not an isolated contribution of world-model prediction.

Part III — Learning, Simulation, and Decision Making

Five consumers, five different endpoints

Predictions can change command selection, policy parameters, action decoding, or correction after new observations. The consumer determines what evidence would be relevant — and what a reported result cannot support.

Learned simulation

Advances predicted states. Assessed by rollout accuracy or behaviour in simulated interaction.

rollout error

Policy learning

Uses imagined transitions, returns, or demonstrations to update parameters. Endpoint is the trained policy's task performance.

task return

Policy evaluation

Estimates a policy's outcomes or relative quality. Agreement must be tested against outcomes observed independently of the evaluator.

agreement

Planning and selection

Chooses among actions. Assessed by task outcomes under a specified protocol; offline ranking proxies are not physical execution.

task success

Execution-time correction

Uses predicted risk or discrepancy to trigger or revise behaviour. Detection, revision, and recovery are distinct endpoints.

latency / recovery

Not a sixth consumer

Reasoner-assisted revision can change a proposal without querying predicted consequences. Astra reports 48% versus 26% direct success on ten RoboDojo tasks — a boundary comparator, not a prediction consumer.

boundary case

One model may serve several uses — but evidence from one does not establish benefit in another. This is the organising rule for the rest of the survey.

3.1 Planning with predicted consequences

Consequence planning must both propose a useful motion and recognise its value before committing. PETS propagates sampled trajectories through probabilistic dynamics ensembles and uses CEM; PlaNet runs CEM on predicted latent states and rewards without rendering images for scoring; TD-MPC2 guides latent search with a learned policy prior and a terminal value. Ensemble uncertainty, a visual belief, and policy/value guidance change different parts of the decision — not merely alternative forecast formats.

3.2 Driving and navigation

Three interfaces appear: searching supplied candidates, conditioning action generation on predicted context, and refreshing decisions from feedback. In the first, prediction changes a ranking; in the second it supplies features or training targets; in the third the next computation is anchored either to generated history or to the executed world. These distinctions determine which ablation explains a reported gain.

3.3 Policy learning with imagined experience

What the optimizer consumes determines how imagination changes learning. Original Dreamer differentiates imagined returns through fixed latent dynamics; MBPO supplies short predicted transitions to SAC updates; DreamerV3 uses normalized REINFORCE. MBPO's Hopper ablations retain much of the benefit with one-step branches, degrade with long branches, and outperform extra no-model SAC updates — so transition accumulation, return truncation, and state information are different weaknesses.

3.4 Memory, failure detection, and recovery

Memory preserves context; recovery changes what happens after a failure. A history-conditioned forecast can support simulation, and a history-conditioned actor can maintain task progress, without either constructing a response to an impending failure. Mem-World's policy-evaluation experiment reports imagined–real success correlation of r = 0.97 — evaluator agreement, not an online recovery endpoint.

3.5 Visual plans and joint world–action models

Predictive supervision and runtime future information answer different questions. Fast-WAM makes the stage distinction explicit: its base action tokens read features of the current observation, not future-video tokens, and removing video co-training leaves architecture/inference unchanged while costing 8.0 RoboTwin and 4.1 LIBERO points. Executing only decoded actions does not remove future tokens from sampling, so information available during denoising and outputs retained afterwards are distinct descriptions of one system.

Part IV — Evaluation, Safety, and Reliability

Six checks, none of which substitutes for another

Evaluation needs a separate map of what a result establishes. These are claim-dependent checks, not six mandatory sequential gates or a single aggregate score.

L1 · Perceptual fidelity

Visual coherence of the predicted future.

L2 · State and physical fidelity

Task-relevant dynamics, geometry, or contact state.

L3 · Action realization

Does changing a supplied command produce the corresponding motion?

L4 · Interaction grounding

Does that motion produce the corresponding environmental response?

L5 · Planning and policy utility

Effects on action choice or learning under a defined consumer and protocol.

L6 · Operational reliability

Failures, intervention timing, recovery, and constraints during execution.

The chain to watch: commanded action → realized motion → environment response → new observation. An L1 or L2 gain does not establish an L5 benefit, and a policy-evaluation correlation is not a newly trained policy's success rate.

4.2 Formal guarantees need physical interfaces

Safety mechanisms intervene at different operations: exploration, policy optimisation, command acceptance, or alarm generation. Classical certificates establish a response under explicit assumptions — confidence-bounded Lyapunov learning restricts exploration to an invariant region; predictive safety filtering tests a backup trajectory; a control-barrier-function QP constrains the instantaneous derivative.

Each requires a valid state/uncertainty model plus, respectively, an initially stabilising policy, a feasible terminal backup, or an admissible safe input. A visual forecast supplies none of these automatically. An alarm is also not a completed intervention.

4.3 Reliability is the consequence, not the timing

FutureRTC isolates action-index alignment under an oracle context: the first generated action yields 96.6% success, while starting at index k = 20 after the same delay yields 0.9%. This is an oracle-context diagnostic, not evidence that the model can access that future observation when it generates the action.

Intervention can itself cause failure: VLA-FixBench's automatic judgers achieve 0–25% completion versus 30% policy-only and 65% human intervention on two physical Franka tasks.

Why a fourth table is missing

The manuscript's per-model interface matrix (its Table 2) marks, for each system, whether it answers supplied actions, generates goal-conditioned futures, or exposes explicit structure. Those marks are typeset as column-positioned glyphs, so reproducing them faithfully requires the original column geometry. This page therefore describes the interface distinction in prose and four verified examples above, rather than re-typesetting a matrix whose cells cannot be checked line by line.

The same logic governs the evaluation and safety audits in the manuscript (its Tables 5 and 6): they are reference tables for readers comparing protocols, not claims that belong on a summary page.

Three validation choices the evidence supports

GNS's recursive robustness cannot replace ContactWorld's goal-scored planning endpoint; DexTouch-WM's evaluator ranking does not select a training-data generator independently of the learner, as IWS's mixtures also illustrate; and FutureRTC's action-index alignment differs from TacPAC's access to a predicted tactile reference. Existing surveys establish the broader evaluation and attribution principles; the contribution here is to connect specific controls and adverse outcomes to these distinct choices.

Open problems

What this survey cannot settle

Each item is a testable gap, in the form the survey argues future work should adopt.

Unobserved candidates

Rejected candidates' physical outcomes are unobserved. Until a protocol branches reproducible failure states while fixing candidate set, continuation policy, horizon and stochastic repeats, selection regret cannot be estimated from executed runs.

Learner-dependent utility

Evaluator agreement does not select a training-data generator independently of the learner. A single correlation statistic cannot stand in for the downstream recipe.

Mixed runtime endpoints

Correction access, action-index alignment, and update timing are distinct operations. No reported study collapses them into a single runtime-efficiency claim.

Guarantees without interfaces

Formal certificates assume a valid state/uncertainty model plus a stabilising policy, a feasible backup, or an admissible safe input. A visual forecast provides none of these.

Intervention harms

Automatic repair pipelines scored below policy-only baselines on physical tasks. Reliability requires measuring the consequence of an intervention, not only its timing.

Sampling bias

The Core Evidence Set is mechanism-focused, not a representative sample. The prevalence of benefits and failures across the field cannot be inferred from it, and inclusion rationales were recorded retrospectively.
