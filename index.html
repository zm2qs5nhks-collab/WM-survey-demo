World Models for Physical AI: Learning to Predict, Plan, and Act in the Real World

Differences in representations, action interfaces, and evaluation protocols obscure when better predictions yield better decisions. This survey reviews data foundations and modelling approaches across robotics, navigation, and autonomous driving, then examines three gaps between what a world model predicts and what a downstream consumer actually gains.

Abstract

Better predictions, unclear decisions

World models, which predict how environments evolve under actions, increasingly support simulation, policy learning, planning, and evaluation in physical AI. However, differences in representations, action interfaces, and evaluation protocols obscure when better predictions yield better decisions.

Drawing on experiments and counterexamples, we examine gaps between recursive accuracy and planning utility, evaluator agreement and policy-training outcomes, and predictive information and execution-time correction. We organise evidence across five uses, identify unresolved attribution questions, and discuss requirements for reliable, action-grounded world modelling.

Part I — Foundations and Scope

Three axes before any performance claim

A world model can be situated along three descriptive axes. They describe an interface, not a ranking of model families or a newly exhaustive taxonomy.

Prediction target Pixels, latent state, objects and geometry, or physical variables
Action query Passive, supplied candidates, goal-conditioned, or joint
Prediction consumer Simulation, learning, evaluation, planning, or correction

Read left to right: what is predicted, what can be asked of it, and where the forecast is finally spent. Most contested claims in the field differ at the third node, not the first.

The action query is the axis that gets confused

Output modality does not tell you what a model can be asked. V-JEPA 2 freezes an action-free encoder and trains a predictor on synchronized observations and end-effector changes, so it answers supplied candidate actions. UniPi instead generates task-conditioned video and grounds it through a separate inverse-dynamics decoder, then executes open-loop. GNS advances a supplied physical state without accepting new candidate-command queries, and Dreamer trains an actor on returns imagined in latent space while exposing no executable action query at all.

Prediction, policy, and control are computational roles, not necessarily separate networks. Action generation alone does not establish consequence prediction.

Part II — Data, Representations, and Predictive Models

What supervision actually licenses

Predicting observed motion and predicting a robot command's consequence require different information. The comparison follows what is available for simulation, learning, and action selection — not representation richness alone.

Commanded action Supplied by the policy
Realized motion L3 — action realization
Environment response L4 — interaction grounding
New observation Becomes the next input

This chain is where most data claims break. Synchronized corpora establish the first link; physical labels and interactive environments supply the later ones. A shared storage format aligns none of them.

The intervention gap in data

Passive video, synchronized commands, physical-state labels, and recovery annotations supply different supervision. DROID broadens scenes on shared hardware; Open X-Embodiment pools bodies with coarsely aligned end-effector actions but retains frame and control differences. Pooling more trajectories can therefore change which transition a training pair describes rather than simply broadening coverage.

Video as feedback, not just forecast

Returning generated observations to an agent changes the consumer even when the predictor is unchanged. In Interactive World Simulator, teleoperators supply commands to collect synthetic demonstrations, whereas policy evaluation feeds generated observations back to a policy. The first interface creates training examples; the second lets the tested policy choose its subsequent inputs.

Latent and structured representations

A representation matters through the distinctions its consumer needs: derivatives for an integrator, attainable geometric change for inverse dynamics, or anticipated deformation for contact control. A compact encoder is not yet a queryable transition. With supplied physical state, graphs organise a numerical update rather than discover objects — MeshGraphNets combines mesh and spatial-contact connectivity and integrates node outputs, optionally remeshing.

Generalization across scenes and embodiments

Three bridges coexist — transferring experience, adapting executable actions, and preserving selected outputs — but their evidence concerns different changes rather than a single generalization score. Sim-to-real WAM adapts Cosmos Policy with roughly 800 simulated demonstrations per task and reports 35% mean zero-shot success on four Franka tasks. That measures the complete transfer recipe, not an isolated contribution of world-model prediction.

Part III — Learning, Simulation, and Decision Making

Five consumers, five different endpoints

The consumer determines what evidence would be relevant — and what a reported result cannot support. One model may serve several uses, but evidence from one does not establish benefit in another.

Simulation Rollout accuracy
Policy learning Trained policy's return
Evaluation Agreement with held-out outcomes
Planning Task success under a protocol
Correction Detection, revision, recovery

Each link is measured by a different quantity. Offline ranking proxies are not physical execution, and a policy-evaluation correlation is not a newly trained policy's success rate.

Planning with predicted consequences

Consequence planning must both propose a useful motion and recognise its value before committing. PETS propagates sampled trajectories through probabilistic dynamics ensembles and uses CEM; PlaNet runs CEM on predicted latent states and rewards without rendering images for scoring; TD-MPC2 guides latent search with a learned policy prior and a terminal value. Ensemble uncertainty, a visual belief, and policy/value guidance change different parts of the decision — not merely alternative forecast formats.

Driving and navigation

Three interfaces appear: searching supplied candidates, conditioning action generation on predicted context, and refreshing decisions from feedback. In the first, prediction changes a ranking; in the second it supplies features or training targets; in the third the next computation is anchored either to generated history or to the executed world. These distinctions determine which ablation explains a reported gain.

Policy learning with imagined experience

What the optimizer consumes determines how imagination changes learning. Dreamer differentiates imagined returns through fixed latent dynamics; MBPO supplies short predicted transitions to SAC updates; DreamerV3 uses normalized REINFORCE. MBPO's Hopper ablations retain much of the benefit with one-step branches, degrade with long branches, and outperform extra no-model SAC updates — so transition accumulation, return truncation, and state information are different weaknesses.

Memory, failure detection, and recovery

Memory preserves context; recovery changes what happens after a failure. A history-conditioned forecast can support simulation, and a history-conditioned actor can maintain task progress, without either constructing a response to an impending failure. Mem-World's policy-evaluation experiment reports imagined–real success correlation of r = 0.97 — evaluator agreement, not an online recovery endpoint.

Visual plans and joint world–action models

Predictive supervision and runtime future information answer different questions. Fast-WAM's base action tokens read features of the current observation, not future-video tokens, and removing video co-training leaves architecture and inference unchanged while costing 8.0 RoboTwin and 4.1 LIBERO points. Executing only decoded actions does not remove future tokens from sampling.

Boundary case, not a sixth consumer. Reasoner-assisted revision can change a proposal without querying predicted environmental consequences. Astra reports 48% versus 26% direct success on ten RoboDojo tasks — a comparator that sharpens the definition of a prediction consumer rather than extending the list.

Part IV — Evaluation, Safety, and Reliability

Six checks, none of which substitutes for another

Evaluation needs a separate map of what a result establishes. These are claim-dependent checks, not six mandatory sequential gates or a single aggregate score.

L1Perceptual fidelity
L2State and physical fidelity
L3Action realization
L4Interaction grounding
L5Planning and policy utility
L6Operational reliability

Not a ladder. The arrows mark the order in which a claim travels, not a requirement to pass every level. An L1 or L2 gain does not establish an L5 benefit; a policy-evaluation correlation is not a newly trained policy's success rate.

Formal guarantees need physical interfaces

Safety mechanisms intervene at different operations: exploration, policy optimisation, command acceptance, or alarm generation. Confidence-bounded Lyapunov learning restricts exploration to an invariant region; predictive safety filtering tests a backup trajectory; a control-barrier-function QP constrains the instantaneous derivative. Each requires a valid state and uncertainty model plus, respectively, a stabilising policy, a feasible terminal backup, or an admissible safe input.

A visual forecast supplies none of these automatically, and an alarm is not a completed intervention.

Reliability is the consequence, not the timing

FutureRTC isolates action-index alignment under an oracle context: the first generated action yields 96.6% success, while starting at index k = 20 after the same delay yields 0.9%. This is an oracle-context diagnostic, not evidence that the model can access that future observation when it generates the action. Intervention can itself cause failure: VLA-FixBench's automatic judgers achieve 0–25% completion versus 30% policy-only and 65% human intervention on two physical Franka tasks.

Synthesis

What the evidence supports, and what is still unrun

Two tables from the manuscript carry the empirical claim. They are reproduced here as placeholders and will be replaced with screenshots of the original tables.

Why no other tables. The per-model interface matrix in the manuscript marks, for each system, whether it answers supplied actions, generates goal-conditioned futures, or exposes explicit structure. Reproducing those marks faithfully needs the original column geometry, so the distinction is described in prose above instead. The evaluation and safety audits are reference tables for readers comparing protocols, not claims that belong on a summary page.

Three validation choices the evidence supports

GNS's recursive robustness cannot replace ContactWorld's goal-scored planning endpoint; DexTouch-WM's evaluator ranking does not select a training-data generator independently of the learner, as IWS's mixtures also illustrate; and FutureRTC's action-index alignment differs from TacPAC's access to a predicted tactile reference. Existing surveys establish the broader evaluation and attribution principles; the contribution here is to connect specific controls and adverse outcomes to these distinct choices.

Open problems

What this survey cannot settle

Each item is a testable gap, in the form the survey argues future work should adopt.

01

Unobserved candidates

Rejected candidates' physical outcomes are unobserved. Until a protocol branches reproducible failure states while fixing candidate set, continuation policy, horizon and repeats, selection regret cannot be estimated from executed runs.

02

Learner-dependent utility

Evaluator agreement does not select a training-data generator independently of the learner. A single correlation statistic cannot stand in for the downstream recipe.

03

Mixed runtime endpoints

Correction access, action-index alignment, and update timing are distinct operations. No reported study collapses them into a single runtime-efficiency claim.

04

Guarantees without interfaces

Formal certificates assume a valid state and uncertainty model plus a stabilising policy, a feasible backup, or an admissible safe input. A visual forecast provides none of these.

05

Intervention harms

Automatic repair pipelines scored below policy-only baselines on physical tasks. Reliability requires measuring the consequence of an intervention, not only its timing.

06

Sampling bias

The Core Evidence Set is mechanism-focused, not a representative sample. The prevalence of benefits and failures cannot be inferred from it, and inclusion rationales were recorded retrospectively.
