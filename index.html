World Models for Physical AI: Learning to Predict, Plan, and Act in the Real World

A critical survey of predictive world models for embodied agents. We trace how prediction enters simulation, policy learning, planning, and execution-time correction — and examine the experimental evidence for when predictive capability actually improves downstream decisions, and when it does not.

Abstract

What this survey argues

World models, which predict how environments evolve under actions, increasingly support simulation, policy learning, planning, and evaluation in physical AI. However, differences in representations, action interfaces, and evaluation protocols obscure when better predictions yield better decisions.

This survey reviews data foundations, modeling approaches, and applications in robotics, navigation, and autonomous driving. Drawing on experiments and counterexamples, we examine three gaps — between recursive accuracy and planning utility, between evaluator agreement and policy-training outcomes, and between predictive information and execution-time correction — then identify the attribution questions that remain open.

The organising claim is deliberately narrow: a gain in prediction fidelity does not establish a gain in decision utility, and the evidence that would show otherwise is often missing from the reported experiment rather than merely inconclusive.

Research landscape

Six directions, grouped by what they serve

Representative systems sorted by publication period and prediction consumer. Read the columns first, then the years: a direction becomes viable when its column appears, not when its first paper is published.

Grouped by consumer, not architecture

Four columns generate actions — predict-then-act, shared backbone, unified VLA, latent world modeling. Two serve as learned environments: policy learning and policy evaluation.

Roles are nonexclusive

PlaNet and Dreamer appear both under predict-then-act and under latent world modeling. One system can occupy several columns, which is why a single taxonomy of models fails.

Where prediction enters

Three interfaces, three different things measured

The same predictor can feed an action head, an evaluator, or a runtime corrector — and each consumer is scored by a different quantity. The three figures below follow that order.

World–action interfaces

What changes between these designs is not whether prediction is trained, but what the action head can read. Removing video co-training leaves architecture and inference unchanged, yet costs 8.0 RoboTwin and 4.1 LIBERO points and drops physical success to 10%.

Base: the prediction head is training-only

Action tokens read current-observation features, not future-video tokens. The 8.0 and 4.1 point losses exceed the spread between configurations of the same family.

Joint and IDM: two ways to use the future

Joint denoises video and action together; IDM generates future representations before actions. Physically IDM leads the family on success while Base leads on completion time, and a pretrained π0.5 leads on both.

V-JEPA Policy: context keys, but no command query

The action expert reads context-position keys and values updated by learnable future queries, not final latents. Observation, instruction and proprioception condition this path; candidate robot commands do not query it.

Latency is a third endpoint

Base and IDM run at 190 versus 810 ms. That figure is separate from completion time and from eventual success, so none of the three can stand in for another.

Evaluation

One model can be the better evaluator and the worse data generator. Both panels use the same study and the same four tasks; they differ only in what they rank.

Panel (a) ranks the evaluator

WM-Mix reaches r = .844 and WM-Robot .646 as a mean over four tasks. If evaluator agreement were the criterion, WM-Mix would be selected.

Panel (b) ranks the training data

WM-Robot beats WM-Mix on π0.5 (0.563 vs 0.494) and X-VLA (0.506 vs 0.400), and loses on FTP-1 (0.688 vs 0.694). Two of three learners favour the low-agreement generator.

Execution-time correction

Temporal alignment decides which actions to execute; a predictive reference decides whether the remaining ones can be corrected. Two mechanisms, often reported as one latency effect.

FutureRTC: anticipate when the action will run

The next chunk is generated against an anticipated execution-time context instead of the stale observation time. Under a fixed oracle context, the choice of index moves success from 96.6% to 0.9%.

TacPAC: correct only what has not run

The committed prefix stays; the unexecuted suffix is revised using fresh tactile feedback and a cached tactile-action reference. Success rises 47 → 64% while Bottle stays at 40 / 40%.

Open problems

What this survey cannot settle

Each item is a testable gap, stated the way the survey argues future work should state them.

01

Unobserved candidates

Rejected candidates' physical outcomes are never observed, so selection regret stays unmeasurable until a protocol branches failure states while fixing candidate set, continuation policy, horizon and repeats.

02

Guarantees without interfaces

Formal certificates assume a valid state and uncertainty model plus a stabilising policy, a feasible backup, or an admissible safe input. A visual forecast provides none of them.

03

Intervention harms

Automatic repair pipelines score below policy-only baselines on physical tasks (0–25% against 30% policy-only, 65% human). Reliability needs the consequence of an intervention, not only its timing.

04

Sampling bias

The Core Evidence Set is mechanism-focused, not representative. The prevalence of benefits and failures cannot be inferred from it, and inclusion rationales were recorded retrospectively.
